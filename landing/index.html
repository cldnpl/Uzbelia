How a lesson works

One lesson, five rounds, each one a different skill.

A lesson isn't done after the first try. You come back to it five times, and every round trains something else. From the second round on, the sentences change too: you get the ones you've seen least.

1

Reading

Match pairs, pick the meaning, fill the gap, build the sentence from tiles.

11 questions
2

Listening

Audio only. Pick what you heard, rebuild it, then write it down as a dictation.

10 questions
3

Writing

Eight sentences on the keyboard and one short text of your own, corrected line by line.

9 questions
4

Speaking

Read aloud, then listen to a native voice and repeat after it.

9 questions
5

Review

Everything mixed, no hints, more of your own words. Then the path moves on.

11 questions

The label is a promise. A round called Listening has only listening in it. Can't talk right now? Tap it, and the speaking round turns into a review and says so.

Calls with Anorcha

Pick a level. The phone rings. You talk.

Anorcha is a pomegranate (anor in Uzbek) and your conversation partner. A call is a real back-and-forth: each question follows from what you just said.

Answer however you like. Speak or type. No multiple choice, no hints. "I don't know" is always an option.

No red marks mid-sentence. The corrections wait for the recap at the end, answer by answer.

It only corrects what it can explain. Spelling, grammar, a more natural way to say it, and why.

In every unit

More than flashcards and taps.

Write to Anorcha

One chapter per unit is a chat. Anorcha writes in your new language, you answer in your own words. Four messages at A1, six at B2, and a full report at the end.

Grammar written for that language

Notes explain each language on its own terms. Thai doesn't conjugate. Persian links words with the ezafe. Uzbek says "I am" with an ending: -man.

Start at your level

Already know some? A placement test finds where you are, and a checkpoint test lets you skip a unit you already know.
